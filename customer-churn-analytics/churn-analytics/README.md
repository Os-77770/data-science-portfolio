# Customer Churn Analytics Pipeline

An end-to-end data analytics project: from raw customer data to SQL analysis,
exploratory data analysis, a machine-learning churn prediction model, and
business recommendations a retention team could act on.

> **Data honesty note:** The dataset used here is **100% synthetic**. It was
> generated by `src/generate_data.py` with a fixed random seed (42) to mimic
> realistic telco customer behaviour — no real customer data is used anywhere
> in this project. All findings below are real outputs of running this code
> on that synthetic dataset.

---

## Business Problem

For a subscription business, acquiring a new customer costs far more than
keeping an existing one — yet most companies only learn a customer was unhappy
after they've already cancelled. This project answers three questions:

1. **Who is churning?** Which customer segments have the highest churn rates?
2. **Why?** Which behaviours (support calls, late payments, contract type…)
   are most strongly associated with churn?
3. **Can we predict it early enough to act?** Build a model that flags
   at-risk customers so a retention team can intervene before they leave.

## Dataset

8,000 synthetic telco-style customers, one row per customer:

| Column | Description |
|---|---|
| `customer_id` | Unique customer ID |
| `tenure_months` | Months with the company (1–72) |
| `monthly_charges` | Monthly bill ($) |
| `total_charges` | Lifetime spend ($) |
| `contract_type` | Month-to-month / One year / Two year |
| `payment_method` | Electronic check, mailed check, auto bank transfer, auto credit card |
| `internet_service` | Fiber optic / DSL / None |
| `support_calls` | Support calls in the observation window |
| `late_payments` | Number of late payments |
| `paperless_billing`, `senior_citizen` | Binary flags |
| `churn` | Target: 1 = churned, 0 = retained |

Overall churn rate in the data: **30.2%** (2,417 of 8,000 customers).

## Tech Stack

## Key Findings (from the SQL + EDA runs)

- **Contract type is the single biggest segment-level driver.** Month-to-month
  customers churn at **37.6%**, vs 22.8% on one-year and **18.3%** on two-year
  contracts. Month-to-month customers are 4,446 of 8,000 customers but account
  for 1,670 of 2,417 churners (~69%).
- **The first year is the danger zone.** Customers in their first 12 months
  churn at **47.8%**; churn falls steadily with tenure to just **9.5%** for
  customers past 49 months. Tenure has the strongest single correlation with
  churn of any numeric feature (r = −0.28).
- **Support pain shows up in churn.** Customers with 3+ support calls churn
  at **39.0%**, vs 23.1% for customers who never called support.
- **Late payments are an early-warning signal.** Churn rises from 24.5%
  (no late payments) to **38.3%** (2+ late payments).
- **Churners pay more per month but stay for less time.** Churned customers
  averaged $62.76/month vs $58.09 for retained customers, but their average
  tenure was 22.0 months vs 33.7 — so their lifetime value was much lower
  ($1,384 avg total charges vs $1,994).
- **Revenue at stake:** the 2,417 churned customers represent **$151,693 in
  monthly recurring revenue** (~$1.82M annualised) in this dataset.

## Model Results

Two models were trained on an 80/20 stratified split, validated with 5-fold
cross-validation, with `class_weight='balanced'` to handle the ~70/30
retained/churned imbalance:

| Model | CV ROC-AUC | Accuracy | Precision | Recall | F1 | ROC-AUC (test) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Logistic Regression | 0.7595 | 0.6744 | 0.4740 | **0.7184** | **0.5712** | **0.7467** |
| Random Forest | 0.7166 | 0.6813 | 0.4759 | 0.5528 | 0.5115 | 0.7073 |

**The simpler model won.** Logistic regression beat the random forest on
ROC-AUC, F1, and — most importantly for a retention use case — recall: it
catches ~72% of customers who will churn. That's a useful lesson in itself:
when relationships are mostly additive and monotonic (more support calls →
more churn, longer tenure → less churn), a linear model can generalise better
than a flexible ensemble on tabular data of this size.

**Top random-forest features:** total charges (0.224), tenure months (0.213),
monthly charges (0.195), support calls (0.086), late payments (0.066).

## Business Recommendations

1. **Attack first-year churn.** With a 47.8% churn rate in year one, add a
   structured onboarding programme: proactive check-ins at months 1, 3, and 6,
   a first-bill review, and a "new customer" support fast lane.
2. **Move month-to-month customers onto term contracts.** Offer a meaningful
   incentive (e.g., two months free or a price lock) to switch to a one-year
   plan — the churn gap (37.6% → 22.8%) dwarfs the cost of the incentive.
3. **Treat support calls as a retention trigger, not just a cost.** Any
   customer hitting 3 support calls should automatically enter a save-play:
   follow-up call, service health check, and a goodwill credit where fitting.
4. **Use late payments as an early-warning flag.** A second late payment
   should trigger outreach *before* the next billing cycle — payment-plan
   options and autopay enrolment (with a small discount) reduce both late
   payments and churn.
5. **Score the base monthly with the churn model.** Run the logistic
   regression over the customer base each month, and give the retention team
   the top-risk decile with the reason codes (contract, tenure, support
   history) so outreach is personal, not generic.

## What I Learned

- **SQL and Python are strongest together**: SQL is the fastest way to answer
  structured business questions on a clean table; Python takes over for
  visualisation and modelling. Building the cleaned analytics table once, in
  SQL, made every downstream step simpler.
- **Class imbalance changes which metric matters.** With ~70% of customers
  retained, a lazy model can score ~70% accuracy by predicting "no churn" for
  everyone — while catching zero churners. Recall and ROC-AUC tell the real
  story for a retention problem.
- **A fancier model is not automatically a better model.** The random forest
  lost to logistic regression here; checking a simple, interpretable baseline
  first is not a formality, it's the control experiment.
- **Feature importance needs scepticism.** Total charges ranks #1 largely
  because it blends tenure and price (it's roughly tenure × monthly charges).
  Reading importance scores without thinking about how features are built
  leads to bad conclusions.
- **Reproducibility is a habit, not a feature**: fixed seeds everywhere mean
  anyone can clone this repo and get exactly these numbers.

## Next Steps (if this were a real engagement)

- Add time-based validation (train on earlier months, test on later ones) to
  simulate predicting the future honestly.
- Tune the decision threshold against an explicit cost model (cost of a
  retention offer vs value of a saved customer).
- Add SHAP explanations so each flagged customer comes with human-readable
  reasons.
- Wrap the pipeline in a scheduled job that refreshes scores monthly.
